# Electrostatics {#electrostatics}

## Definition {#definition}
- Interaction between electric charges that are at rest
- Opposite charges attract, like charges repel

## Types of Charges {#types-of-charges}
- Protons (+)
- Neutrons (uncharged)
- Electrons (-)

## Atomic Structure {#atomic-structure}
- Neutral atoms have a matching number of protons and electrons
	- The number of protons/electrons is called the <a class="internal-link" href="/notes/phys-2212/atomic-number/">atomic number</a>
- An <a class="internal-link" href="/notes/phys-2212/ion/">ion</a> is an atom that has gained or lost electrons
- <a class="internal-link" href="/notes/phys-2212/ionization/">Ionization</a> is the process of gaining/losing electrons

## Conservation of charge {#conservation-of-charge}
- <a class="internal-link" href="/notes/phys-2212/conservation-of-charge/">Conservation of charge</a> states that the sum of electric charges in any closed system is constant
- Charge is <a class="internal-link" href="/notes/phys-2212/quantized/">quantized</a> with the magnitude of the charge of a proton/electron as the unit of charge

## Coulomb's Law {#coulomb-s-law}

- <div class="transclusion" data-source="04. Notes/Undergraduate Courses/PHYS 2212/Coulomb&#x27;s Law.md">
  <a class="transclusion-source" href="/notes/phys-2212/coulomb-s-law/" title="Open Coulomb&#x27;s Law"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-link"><path d="M10 13a5 5 0 0 0 7.54.54l3-3a5 5 0 0 0-7.07-7.07l-1.72 1.71"/><path d="M14 11a5 5 0 0 0-7.54-.54l-3 3a5 5 0 0 0 7.07 7.07l1.71-1.71"/></svg></a>

  The magnitude of the electric force between two point charges is:

  $$
  F = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{\vert q_{1} q_{2}\vert}{r^2}
  $$

  $k$ is often substituted for $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}, \sim 9\mathrm{e}9$ Nm^2/C^2
  $\epsilon_0 \sim 8.85\mathrm{e}-12$ C^2/Nm^2

  <a class="zoom" href="/notes/img/coulombs-law.png"><img src="/notes/img/coulombs-law.png" alt="coulombs law" loading="lazy"></a>

  </div>

- SI unit for charge is the Coulomb (C) with 1 e $\sim 1.602\mathrm{e}-19$ C
- SI unit for current (moving charge) is the Ampere (A), or Coulombs per second

### Superposition Principle {#superposition-principle}
- The total force on a charge in a group of charges is a vector sum of the electric forces the other charges exert on that charge individually

## Electric Field and Electric Forces {#electric-field-and-electric-forces}
### Electric Field {#electric-field}

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{\vec{F}_{0}}{q_{0}}
$$

- A point charge creates an electric field ($\vec{E}$) in the surrounding space
	- $\vec{E}$ is not a single vector but a vector field
- Electric fields from other charges create the electric force on a charge

- <div class="transclusion" data-source="04. Notes/Undergraduate Courses/PHYS 2212/Uniform field.md">
  <a class="transclusion-source" href="/notes/phys-2212/uniform-field/" title="Open Uniform field"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-link"><path d="M10 13a5 5 0 0 0 7.54.54l3-3a5 5 0 0 0-7.07-7.07l-1.72 1.71"/><path d="M14 11a5 5 0 0 0-7.54-.54l-3 3a5 5 0 0 0 7.07 7.07l1.71-1.71"/></svg></a>

  A uniform $\vec{E}$ has a constant magnitude and direction within a region

  </div>

- We always draw electric fields as though the test charge is (+)
- We assume the test charge $q_0$ is infinitesimally small
- $\vec{F}_0$ points in the direction of $\vec{E}$ if $q_0$ is positive and vice-versa

#### $\vec{E}$ Superposition {#e-superposition}
For a group of point charges, the E field at $P$ is:

$$
\begin{align*}
\vec{F}_{0} &= \vec{F}_{1} + \vec{F}_{2} + \vec{F}_{3} + \dots \\
&= q_{0}\vec{E}_{1} + q_{0}\vec{E}_{2} + q_{0}\vec{E}_{3} + \dots \\
&= q_{0}(\vec{E}_{1} + \vec{E}_{2} + \vec{E}_{3} + \dots ) \\
&= q_{0}\left( \sum_{i=1}^n \vec{E}_{i} \right)
\end{align*}
$$

where $\vec{E}_0$ may be substituted in for $\sum_{i=1}^n \vec{E}_{i}$. Alternative formula is $$
E_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \vec{E}_{i} = \frac{\vec{F}_0}{q_0}$$
<a class="zoom" href="/notes/img/e-field-superposition.png"><img src="/notes/img/e-field-superposition.png" alt="e field superposition" loading="lazy"></a>

#### Electric Field Lines {#electric-field-lines}
- Imaginary lines whose tangents at $P$ are in the direction of $\vec{E}_P$
- The magnitude of $\vec{E}$ is stronger with closer spaced lines
- The lines never intersect, so the direction of $\vec{E}_P$ is unique
- $\vert \vec{E} \vert$ varies at different points

## Electric Dipoles {#electric-dipoles}

- <div class="transclusion" data-source="04. Notes/Undergraduate Courses/PHYS 2212/dipole.md">
  <a class="transclusion-source" href="/notes/phys-2212/dipole/" title="Open dipole"><svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" class="lucide lucide-link"><path d="M10 13a5 5 0 0 0 7.54.54l3-3a5 5 0 0 0-7.07-7.07l-1.72 1.71"/><path d="M14 11a5 5 0 0 0-7.54-.54l-3 3a5 5 0 0 0 7.07 7.07l1.71-1.71"/></svg></a>

  a pair of point charges with equal magnitude and opposite sign

  </div>

- The electric dipole moment ($\vec{p}$) goes from (-) to (+)
	- $\vec{p} = qd$
- $\sum F = 0$ in a uniform E field, but $\sum \tau \ne 0$.

### Net Torque {#net-torque}

$$
\begin{align*}
\tau_{(+)} = r_{\perp}F_{(+)} = \frac{d}{2}(\sin\phi)F \\
\tau_{(-)} = r_{\perp}F_{(-)} = \frac{d}{2}(\sin \phi)F
\end{align*}
$$

Since $\tau_{(+)}$ and $\tau_{(-)}$ act in the same direction: 

$$
\begin{align*}
\sum \tau &= q\vec{E}d(\sin \phi) \\
&= p\vec{E}(\sin \phi) \\
&= \vec{p} \times \vec{E}
\end{align*}
$$

<a class="zoom" href="/notes/img/net-torque-electric-dipole.png"><img src="/notes/img/net-torque-electric-dipole.png" alt="net torque electric dipole" loading="lazy"></a>

### Potential Energy {#potential-energy}

$$
\begin{align*}
dW &= \tau \ \mathrm{d}\phi \\
&= -pE\sin \phi \ \mathrm{d}\phi \\
W &= \int_{\phi_{1}}^{\phi_{2}}-(pE\sin \phi) \ \mathrm{d}\phi \\
&= pE\cos \phi_{2} - pE\cos \phi_{1}
\end{align*}
$$

By the Work-Energy Theorem, $W = -\Delta U$, so $U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE\cos \phi$
